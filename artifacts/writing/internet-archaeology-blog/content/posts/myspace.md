# MySpace

In August 2003, a website launched that let you paint the walls. It was
called MySpace, and its radical feature was mess: your page was yours --
colors, backgrounds, autoplaying music, layout broken in exactly the way you
chose to break it. Customizable profile pages with embedded music became its
signature, and an entire generation of bands measured their worth in friend
counts and play counters. [S1]

It is hard to convey the size. In 2006, at least one major measurement firm
reported MySpace as the most-visited website in the United States -- the
most-visited site in the country, a social network, above the portals. Treat
the superlative with care (measurement firms disagreed), but not the fact:
for a moment the biggest thing on the American web was a pile of homemade
profile pages. [S1]

The money followed the size. In July 2005, News Corporation bought MySpace
for 580 million US dollars -- a number that now reads like a rounding error
and then read like a king's ransom. In June 2011 the site was sold again, to
Specific Media, for a price reported at around 35 million. Read those two
numbers together slowly: 580 in, a reported 35 out. Whatever the exact
figures, six years unwound nearly all of it. [S1]

What killed it is the familiar compound: a cleaner rival, a botched
redesign era, and the plain fact that messy freedom ages badly once your
mother joins. What is worth keeping is what it got right -- the insight that
people want a place they can redecorate. The modern web gave every profile
the same walls; MySpace assumed you were an interior designer, and for years
millions of people were. [S1]

Then came the loss that earns it a place in this gazette. In 2019 it emerged
that Myspace had lost all the music its users uploaded between 2003 and
2015 -- twelve years of demos, basement recordings, and small-band
discographies, gone in what the company described as a server migration.
The Hacker News thread is one of the longest funerals in the collection:
343 points, 182 comments, mostly people listing what they had uploaded and
would never hear again. [S2]

A service dying is one thing. A service dying while holding the only copy
of its users' work is another -- and MySpace is the canonical case, the
reason "the cloud keeps it safe" stopped being an argument anyone could
make with a straight face.

The afterlife is affectionate and small. In 2020 a developer posted a
reconstruction of MySpace as it looked in 2007, and hundreds showed up to
walk through the rebuild like a preserved town. [S3] And Hacker News still
resurfaces artifacts from the peak, like a 2007 essay asking whether MySpace
would ever lose its monopoly -- read today, it is a time capsule of an era
when the question had only one imaginable answer. [S4]

In 2003 the web gave people a page and a paint bucket. In 2019 we learned
the music was gone. The walls, somewhere in the Internet Archive, are still
loud.

**Remembered:** the page you could paint, and the songs it ate.
