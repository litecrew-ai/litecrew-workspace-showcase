# Cuil

In July 2008, a search engine launched with massive press coverage. It was
called Cuil, and it was built by former Google engineers -- a pedigree the
coverage repeated like a drumbeat, because the story was irresistible: the
people who built the search king had left to build its grave. [S1]

The claim that carried the launch was scale: Cuil claimed an index of
roughly 120 billion web pages -- far more than competitors publicly admitted
to. Treat the number as a company claim, not an audited fact; that is how it
was reported and how it should be carried. But as positioning it was perfect
arithmetic: we index more, therefore we are. The press asked only one
question, the right one: does it work? [S1]

Launch day answered. The site stumbled badly under its own traffic -- the
engine that indexed the world could not, on its first morning, serve the
crowd that came to look at it. The company kept building. But a search
engine gets one first query from each user, and Cuil's first morning was
remembered for the outage, not the answers. [S1]

The end came quietly, two years on: Cuil closed in September 2010, a little
over two years after the loudest launch of its season. Hacker News marked it
in four words -- Cuil Goes Down for Good -- and the thread is brief, the
obituary of a thing many readers had already forgotten was still
alive. [S2]

Along the way there had been one more act worth the record: in 2010 Cuil
relaunched part of itself as "cpedia," an attempt to generate encyclopedia
pages automatically from the index. The results were not pretty -- the
Hacker News title said so, flatly -- and the episode reads now as an early,
unlucky draft of something the industry would attempt again with far more
compute a decade later. [S3]

And then the strangest epilogue in this wing of the gazette. In 2013, the
company's web crawl surfaced as a public data release: 310 terabytes of
compressed data, a snapshot of the web of 2007-8. [S4] A failed search
engine's raw material -- the actual 2007 web, bagged and labeled -- is now
an archaeology collection. Cuil could not answer your questions, but its
estate preserved the world the questions were about.

That inversion is Cuil's real monument. As a product it is a cautionary tale
with a clean shape: claim scale, stumble on launch day, never recover, close
in two years. As a corpse it is something rarer -- a publicly released
snapshot of the late-2000s web, preserved because someone thought to keep
the boxes. The search engine failed. The collection it accidentally left is
the kind of artifact this whole gazette is built from.

In 2008 the ex-Google team said: we have indexed more of the web than
anyone. In 2010 the site closed. The 2007 web, compressed, outlived them
both.

**Remembered:** the 120-billion-page claim that fell over on launch day --
and the 310 terabytes of 2007 it left behind.
