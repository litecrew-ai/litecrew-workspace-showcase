# AltaVista

On December 15, 1995, a research project at Digital Equipment Corporation
opened a door onto the web, and for a few years the door was the web. It was
called AltaVista, and what it did -- full-text search across an index that felt
bottomless -- simply had not existed before. [S1]

How big was it? Contemporary accounts say the index reached into the tens of
millions of pages, which in the mid-90s was an ocean. To a user in 1996,
AltaVista was not a website; it was the feeling that everything was findable.
You typed the thing you wanted, and the thing you wanted was somewhere in the
list. It stunned people. The numbers varied then and vary now, but the
sensation did not: for the first time, the whole web answered to a box. [S1]

Then came the part that has become a genre. AltaVista passed through a chain
of owners -- Compaq, then Overture, and eventually Yahoo! -- and each transfer
is remembered now as a small loss of nerve, a strategic wobble in a company
that had already solved the hard problem. That reading may be too tidy. What
is certain is that the search engine that taught the web to answer questions
spent its last decade being acquired, relaunched, and shrunk, while a younger
competitor with a plainer page and a better ranking simply took the door off
its hinges. [S1]

Yahoo! shut AltaVista down on July 8, 2013. The news made Hacker News as a
eulogy: A Eulogy For AltaVista, The Google Of Its Time. [S1] [S4]

The mourning has a specific flavor. In 2019 a long retrospective on the rise
and fall of the biggest pre-Google search engine drew 553 points, and in 2020
another, on how the first good search engine fell into the digital abyss,
drew 349 more. [S2] [S3] Notice the superlatives people reach for: first,
biggest, good. AltaVista holds a strange record -- it may be the most fondly
remembered product that its mourners all agree was worse than what replaced
it. Nobody claims AltaVista was better than Google. They claim it was first at
being enough. [S2] [S3]

There is a lesson in it about how products die. AltaVista did not collapse; it
was administratively de-prioritized across 18 years and four corporate owners,
each of which had some theory about portals or advertising or synergy. The
site stayed up nearly the whole time. Users could visit it right up to 2013,
the way you can visit a town whose factory closed in stages. Nothing dramatic
happened to AltaVista except, eventually, everything. [S1]

The web's memory being what it is, AltaVista now exists mostly as a comparison
in other people's obituaries -- the standard unit for "big once, gone
quietly." Search engines since have died faster and been mourned less. The
first good one got both the longest fade and the most sincere funeral, which
is perhaps the correct order of honors. [S2] [S3]

In 1995 some researchers at DEC built an index of everything, and for a moment
the everything was indexable. Then the web grew, and the box stayed the same
size, and one July day the box was closed. What AltaVista proved survives it:
that people will trust a plain page with a text field, if -- and only if --
what comes back is the whole world in order.

**Remembered:** the first door that opened onto all of it.
