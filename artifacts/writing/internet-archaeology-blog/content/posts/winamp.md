# Winamp

In 1997, a young programmer wrote a program that played MP3s. Justin
Frankel released Winamp in April of that year under his Nullsoft name, and
within months it was sitting in the system tray of every dorm room in
America. [S1]

The program mattered because it was yours. You could skin it: wrap it in
amateur chrome, anime characters, brushed metal, anything the imagination
could misjudge. Winamp made the skinnable interface famous, and for years
it was simply the default way a computer played music. [S1]

And it talked. Boot it up and a sampled voice declared the software's
tagline: it really whips the llama. Ask anyone who was there. They can
still hear it. [S1]

AOL noticed. In 1999 it bought Nullsoft in a deal reported at the time at
roughly 80 to 100 million US dollars, which was, in retrospect, the peak
valuation of a certain dream: that the big dull companies of the cable era
could buy their way into being loved on the internet. They could not. They
bought software; the love was not included in the transaction. [S1]

For a decade Winamp drifted inside AOL like an exhibit in a museum nobody
visited. The kids who had skinned it grew up, the music moved into
streaming clients that could not be skinned by anyone, and on a day in
October 2013 AOL announced the artifact would finally be decommissioned
that December. [S1]

The internet drew the line at llama jokes. When the shutdown reached
Hacker News, the thread carried the specifics in its title -- Winamp
shutting down on December 20th, 2013 -- and drew 957 points of protest,
mourning, and last-minute nostalgia. A twenty-year-old player for a file
format people had stopped needing, and a crowd still gathered. [S2]

Then the strangest thing: the execution was stayed. In January 2014 the
brand was sold to the Radionomy company, and Winamp lived on under other
owners, patched and reissued, a ghost that kept renewing its lease.
Software dies the way software dies, but every so often the paperwork says
no. [S1]

The llama, it turned out, was load-bearing. The MP3 era produced a hundred
players and the internet kept exactly one of them in its heart, the one
that talked back. Nobody holds funerals for codecs or hex editors. They
held one, effectively, for a media player, and the anniversary of its
cancellation still gets marked.

**Remembered:** the player that let you paint it, and the voice that
greeted you when it started.
