# AOL Instant Messenger

In 1997, AOL launched a program whose entire product was the knowledge that
someone else was online. It was called AOL Instant Messenger, and it did one
thing: it kept a list of your friends and told you, at a glance, who was
reachable. [S1]

That list was the invention. Not the protocol, not the client -- the buddy
list. It defined a generation of evening internet use in the United States, and
if you want to understand how, picture the ritual: sign on, watch the window
populate, and begin the evening's negotiation with whoever the machine said
existed. [S1]

Presence is a strange thing to build a life around. Before the buddy list,
being reachable required effort -- a phone call, a knock. After it, absence
became information. A grayed-out name meant something. An away message meant
something else. Ask anyone who was there: the away message, the little note you
left on your own door, was the status update before status updates, a genre
invented by teenagers and later reissued by corporations. [S1]

What made AIM matter was that it was nobody's business plan for your
attention. There was no feed. There was no algorithm deciding who you heard
from. There was a list you wrote yourself, in an order you chose, and the
people on it could reach you or not, and that was the whole system. The
evenings belonged to whoever you had added. [S1]

The end came with a courteous twenty-year notice. In October 2017 AOL
announced that the service would end, and on December 15, 2017, it went dark.
When the shutdown reached Hacker News, the thread title did the math for
anyone who had not: AIM will shut down after 20 years. Twenty years of buddy
lists, and the announcement drew 543 points and 330 comments from people
counting up their own. [S1] [S2]

Read the comments on a shutdown thread some time. They are reunions. Grown
engineers, most of them, trading screen names they have not typed in a decade,
reconstructing who dated whom from the evidence of away messages. Nobody mourns
a protocol. They mourn being nineteen with the whole list online. [S2]

The service's real legacy is grammatical. AIM was where a generation learned
to write to each other in short bursts, to mean things by logging off, to
perform a self in a few lines of a profile. Every chat app since has been a
variation on the buddy list with a business model bolted on. The list itself --
yours, handmade, unordered by anyone's ranking -- has never really come back.

In 1997 AOL shipped a window that told you who was online. In 2017 the window
closed. The friends, presumably, are still out there, greyed out forever,
waiting to be reached by some other means. That is the quiet joke of the
digital graveyard: the buddy list died, the buddies moved on, and only the
machine kept the exact shape of the evening.

**Remembered:** the list you wrote yourself, and the evenings it organized.
