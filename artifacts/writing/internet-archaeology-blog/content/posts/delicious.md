# del.icio.us

In 2003, Joshua Schachter built a website whose name was its own address:
del.icio.us. The idea was almost embarrassingly small. You saved a link. The
link went on a list. The list was public. [S1]

The small idea turned out to be two inventions wearing one trench coat. One
was social bookmarking: your saved links, visible to everyone, became a
collectively maintained index of what the web was worth reading. The other
was tagging. Instead of filing a link under one folder, you attached free-form
words to it, and the tags aggregated across every user into a live taxonomy of
the whole web. del.icio.us pioneered the first and popularized the second, and
the tagging it normalized is now the organizing principle of half the
software you use. [S1]

Yahoo! acquired the service at the end of 2005, which in the mid-2000s was the
internet's way of conferring sainthood. It did not confer longevity. [S1]

The near-death came as a leak. In December 2010 an internal Yahoo! slide --
reported at the time as a sunset plan -- escaped onto the web, and the
reaction was panic and mass export: users scrambling to download years of
their own memory before a corporate organogram finished its sentence. The
slide's exact status was never the point. The point was that its users
discovered, all at once, that their bookmarks were hostages. [S1]

Watch the moment on Hacker News: Yahoo is Shutting Down Del.icio.us, 486
points, 293 comments, and inside those comments something remarkable -- users
coordinating rescues of a URL database the way neighbors pass buckets during a
fire. Nothing about a bookmark is irreplaceable. Everything about a
community's accumulated judgment of the web is. [S2]

The patient survived the fire and died of the transfers. In 2011 Yahoo! sold
the service to AVOS Systems, a company started by YouTube's founders Chad
Hurley and Steve Chen, and the redesigns began. The famous domain was
eventually simplified, which was the moment the older mourners knew the
patient had changed. [S1]

The ending, when it came, was gentle and strange. On June 1, 2017, the
bookmarking service Pinboard acquired del.icio.us and placed it in read-only
mode -- an archive, sealed. Not deleted: frozen. You can still go read what
millions of people thought the web was, in the years when the web was
something you could hold in a list. [S1]

There is a lesson in the freeze. Every dead service in this gazette is a
loss, but most are erasures. del.icio.us is the rare one that ended as a
museum of itself: the links still resolve to wherever they resolve, the tags
still hang over them in their original clouds, and nothing new can be added.
It is a rare entry in this collection whose corpse you can browse.

And the tagging? It outlived the tagger. The folk taxonomy that del.icio.us
taught the web -- unordered, plural, personal -- now runs beneath every
hashtag you have ever typed. The site that invented your vocabulary closed in
2017, read-only, mid-sentence.

**Remembered:** the public list of what the web was worth, and the words we
learned to file it under.
