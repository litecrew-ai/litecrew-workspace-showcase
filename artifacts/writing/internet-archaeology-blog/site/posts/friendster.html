Friendster

The first social network, which deleted its own memory on the way to becoming a games site.

August 29, 2026 -- 591 words -- exhibit no. 007

In 2002, Jonathan Abrams founded a website on a hypothesis: that your real friends, your actual ones, would be a better filter for the internet than anonymous crowds. He called it Friendster, and it was one of the first mainstream social networks -- the proof that "people you know" could be the product. [S1]

Everything later is descended from it. The profile, the list of friends, the web of connections you could wander -- the genre's whole grammar was sketched here first. What is easy to forget is that for a brief window, this specific implementation of the idea was the future, and then the page took eleven seconds to load, and the future moved. [S1]

The scale, later, was enormous and elsewhere. By the late 2000s Friendster claimed well over one hundred million registered users, most of them in Asia -- numbers that come from the company's own claims and should be held as such, but which describe something real: a social network bigger than its reputation in the West, alive in the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia long after America had filed it under "failed." [S1]

Then came the act this gazette exists to record. In 2011 Friendster relaunched as a social gaming service, and ahead of the switch it deleted users' stored photos, blogs, and comments. Not archived, not exported, not migrated: deleted, so that a games site could stand where a decade of ordinary photographs had been. A social network's memories, cleared like a browser cache. [S1]

Hacker News marked the relaunch with two flat words -- Friendster Relaunches -- 52 points of quiet horror. [S3] The remnant service closed a few years later, in 2015 by most accounts. [S1]

The afterlife is the strangest chapter. In 2023 someone was trying to rebuild Friendster from scratch, and the thread about it drew the polite interest reserved for replicas of famous shipwrecks. [S4] Then, in 2026, the ending nobody scripted: a buyer announced on Hacker News -- I bought Friendster for $30k -- and 1,116 points of attention followed. Thirty thousand dollars. The domain that invented the social graph, traded for the price of a used car. [S2]

Read that 2026 thread and you find the full range of the dead-web condition: people who lost teenage photos to the 2011 deletion, people who never forgave the load times, people simply amazed the name was still for sale. The purchase price is the seller's claim, and worth hedging until the paper is public, but as a symbol it needs no verification: the first social network, and the cheapest acquisition in the collection. [S2]

Friendster's real monument is the deletion. Other services in this gazette died of neglect or mergers; this one chose. By its own claims it had a hundred million registered reasons to archive the photographs, and it deleted them instead, which is why "back up your stuff" became the first commandment of the social web. Every export button you have ever clicked is a little memorial to Friendster. [S1]

In 2002 a man built a site where friends could find each other. The site forgot its users on purpose, died twice, and sold for pocket change. The friends, presumably, still find each other.

Remembered: the first map of who knew whom -- and the day the map burned its own photographs.
