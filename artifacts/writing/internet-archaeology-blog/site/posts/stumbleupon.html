StumbleUpon

A button that took you somewhere you never would have gone, and the web that stopped pressing it.

August 29, 2026 -- 540 words -- exhibit no. 018

In 2001, a student named Garrett Camp and his co-founders built a toolbar, and on the toolbar was one of the great buttons in the history of the web. It said Stumble. You pressed it, and it took you somewhere -- a page matched to your interests, chosen by machinery you could not see, and above all a page you had not chosen. [S1]

That was the whole product: serendipity as a button. Not search, where you must already know what you want. Not a feed, where the destination is optimized toward your existing self. The Stumble button sent you to a random page matched to your interests -- the perfect phrase, because it promised both relevance and surprise, the two things every recommendation system since has tried and failed to hold at once. [S1]

For years it worked. People wasted evenings the honest way, one stumble at a time, discovering hobbyist shrines and academic curiosities and personal homepages of strangers who shared exactly one interest with them. The web it navigated was a web of pages, made by people, linked to each other. It is worth saying plainly: the button worked because the web worked. [S1]

On June 30, 2018, StumbleUpon shut down, directing its users to a successor service called Mix. Sixteen years after the student project, the serendipity engine was retired with a forwarding address. The closing announcement drew its own Hacker News thread -- StumbleUpon closing after 16 years -- 279 points of people trading the strange pages they had been shown. [S1] [S2]

Why did it die? The honest answer is that the ground shifted. The button needed an open web of small pages worth stumbling onto; the web that replaced it is a small number of large platforms that do not want you to leave, and a discovery tool that points outward has nothing to point at. Serendipity did not lose to a better serendipity engine. It lost to the enclosure of the thing it browsed. [S1]

The mourners keep trying to rebuild it. In 2020 a Show HN appeared -- StumblingOn, randomly finding indie websites, explicitly recreating StumbleUpon -- and the thread had the tone of people rebuilding a beloved bridge by hand. [S3] It is not the only attempt; it will not be the last. The button is easy to reimplement. The web it pressed through is what went missing.

There is one more epitaph, found in a 2008 Hacker News discussion of how Reddit, StumbleUpon, del.icio.us, and Hacker News ranked their flows: even then, engineers were picking apart the machinery of discovery -- the algorithms that decided what you would accidentally see. [S4] StumbleUpon sat in that lineage, the warmest of them, the only one whose promise was that the machine would be kind about it.

In 2001 a button promised to take you somewhere interesting. In 2018 the button stopped. The somewhere, mostly, went first.

Remembered: the button that chose for you, kindly, and the evenings it organized.
