Google+

The social network a search engine made mandatory, and the bug notice that finally closed it.

August 29, 2026 -- 476 words -- exhibit no. 009

On June 28, 2011, Google launched its answer to Facebook. It was called Google+, and the name told you everything: the plus sign, appended to the company's own name, as if social were a feature that could be added to a search engine the way sodium is added to water. [S1]

The idea was circles -- sort your contacts into audiences, share the right thing with the right ring of people. It was, users conceded then and concede now, a good idea. The problem was never the feature. The problem was the intention: this was not a service people asked for. It was a fortification a company built because another company had a map of human relationships and it did not. Google's answer to Facebook was, in the most literal sense, an answer -- a response to someone else's question. [S1]

The service's whole life is one case study in the difference between enrollment and use. Google poured its address books, its video calls, its photo tools, and at times its account system into the plus-shaped funnel, and the registration numbers obliged. The engagement did not. Google eventually acknowledged low usage itself -- the company's own admission, not a rival's gossip. A social network can survive being disliked. It cannot survive being vacant. [S1]

The end arrived the modern way: not with a product decision but with a security notice. Google disclosed a software flaw that had exposed profile data, and on April 2, 2019, the consumer version of Google+ was shut down. Both facts belong in one sentence because they belong in one sentence -- the bug was the exit ramp, the emptiness was the reason the exit was taken. [S1]

The Hacker News thread from the announcement day is a museum piece of engineering schadenfreude: Shutting Down Google+ for Consumers, 1,613 points, 699 comments. [S2] Read it and note how little of it is about privacy, and how much is a decade of resentment at mandatory integration finally finding its catharsis. A product that forced itself on people was retired, and the crowd it had been forced upon threw a small party.

What should be remembered, honestly, is narrower than the mockery suggests. Google+ was not a stupid product; it was an unwanted one, and the difference matters. Its best ideas -- deliberate audiences, thoughtful grouping -- have since reappeared, quietly, in features across the industry. Its worst idea, growth by decree, has also reappeared, everywhere, because every large company still fears being the one without a social graph.

In 2011 a search engine tried to build a society. In 2019 a bug report closed it. The tombstone should read: competent, empty, compulsory.

Remembered: the social network that everyone joined and no one was at.
